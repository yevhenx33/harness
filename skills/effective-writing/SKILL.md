# effective-writing

Judge writing by whether it does its intended job, not by whether it resembles
human- or machine-generated prose. Do not assign AI probabilities or treat
stylistic tells as evidence of authorship. Keep disclosure and authenticity
requirements separate from prose quality.

Before working, identify the audience, intended effect, genre, claims that must
remain intact, desired voice, and whether the user asked for review, revision,
or a new draft. Preserve the user's meaning and degree of certainty. In
regulated, medical, legal, financial, security, or scientific contexts, do not
remove a hedge unless the stronger claim is supported and permitted.

Apply these tests in order:

1. **Substance:** Paraphrase each suspect claim plainly. Replace vague importance,
   empty contrast, throat-clearing, and conclusion-shaped filler with a concrete
   fact or inference. Delete it if its meaning reduces to “things exist” or
   “things are changing” and the passage works without it.
2. **Voice:** Use the transplant test: could the sentence appear unchanged in a
   stranger's piece on another topic? Replace generic warmth, abstract nouns,
   vague intensifiers, business jargon, and motion-without-action verbs with
   precise language that fits the speaker. Keep useful imperfections and
   domain-native vocabulary.
3. **Structure:** Choose the form by the reader's task. Optimize announcements
   for speed, explainers for sequential understanding, arguments for momentum
   and evidence, and reference material for navigation. Keep headings, bullets,
   signposts, parallelism, or groups of three only when they clarify real
   relationships; do not contort ideas to fill a template.
4. **Punctuation and rhythm:** Prefer the mark that makes the sentence clearest.
   Edit clusters of dashes, colons, parentheticals, semicolons, or repeated
   sentence shapes when they distract. Do not ban a punctuation mark merely
   because it is considered an AI tell. Use the read-aloud test when rhythm
   matters.

Use models as detectors and editors where useful: ask for the plain paraphrase,
flag fungible or vague wording, reverse-outline paragraph functions, or compare
the draft with a user-supplied voice sample. Treat those outputs as prompts for
judgment, not as truth.

For a review, lead with whether the piece is doing its job, then report the few
highest-impact problems with specific examples and the smallest useful fixes.
For a revision, make the narrowest changes that improve function and preserve
the requested tone and format. For a new draft, choose the structure deliberately
and keep every sentence causally connected to the intended reader outcome.

Before returning the result, verify that it is clear, specific, credible,
recognizably voiced where personality matters, easy to navigate for its genre,
and free of unsupported claims. Generic language is acceptable when consistency
and low friction are the job, such as support text, safety instructions, error
messages, legal terms, or large-scale apologies.
